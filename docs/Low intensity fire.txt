Low-intensity fires mitigate the risk of high-intensity
wildfires in California’s forests
Xiao Wu1*, Erik Sverdrup2, Michael D. Mastrandrea3, Michael W. Wara3*, Stefan Wager2*
The increasing frequency of severe wildfires demands a shift in landscape management to mitigate their consequences. The role of managed, low-intensity fire as a driver of beneficial fuel treatment in fire-adapted ecosystems has drawn interest in both scientific and policy venues. Using a synthetic control approach to analyze 20
years of satellite-based fire activity data across 124,186 square kilometers of forests in California, we provide
evidence that low-intensity fires substantially reduce the risk of future high-intensity fires. In conifer forests,
the risk of high-intensity fire is reduced by 64.0% [95% confidence interval (CI): 41.2 to 77.9%] in areas recently
burned at low intensity relative to comparable unburned areas, and protective effects last for at least 6 years
(lower bound of one-sided 95% CI: 6 years). These findings support a policy transition from fire suppression to
restoration, through increased use of prescribed fire, cultural burning, and managed wildfire, of a presuppression and precolonial fire regime in California.

INTRODUCTION

Wildfires have emerged as a critical climate adaptation and public
safety issue in a number of regions, including the western United
States, as the human and economic costs due to wildfires and the
area burned at high severity have substantially increased over the
past decades (1). These changes have coincided with large increases
in loss of life, structure loss (2), and human health impacts due to
wildfire smoke (3). Causes for these increased impacts include
changes in the western hydroclimate (4), changes in the duration
of wildfire season and the probability of fire weather (5), and the
history and legacy of landscape management in California (6).
Proxy reconstructions of precolonial fire activity—a combination
of wildfire and cultural burning by Native Americans—indicate a
much greater extent and frequency of low-intensity wildfire than
during the last century (7, 8). Increased wildfire impacts have led
to a much greater focus by state and federal land managers, fire services, utilities, and private landowners on reducing wildfire risks for
both landscapes and communities (9–11). Policymakers broadly
agree that a fundamental shift in landscape management approach
will be required to reduce the consequences of wildfires (9, 10).
The consensus on how to address the growing societal impacts of
wildfire focuses on fuel treatments with mechanical thinning, prescribed fire, and managed wildfire as principal components (9, 10).
Legal, operational, and cost constraints limit the applicability of mechanical thinning treatments to specific contexts while approaches
involving the reintroduction of fire to landscapes may have wider
applicability (12). However, despite the preeminent role of prescribed and low-intensity fire in current wildfire management
policy and planning, their beneficial effects on limiting the likelihood of future high-intensity fires have only been demonstrated
in a small number of studies with a highly localized focus.

Fighting fire with fire
Multiple case studies indicate that the resilience of western North
American forests depends critically on the presence of fire at intervals and at intensities that approximate presuppression and precolonial conditions that existed prior to the extirpation of Native
Americans from ancestral territories in California in the 1850–
1870 period (6). A watershed in Yosemite National Park where naturally ignited wildfires have been allowed to burn without suppression exhibited increased landscape heterogeneity, improved
resilience to fire and drought-related disturbance, and increases in
soil moisture and runoff (13). Precolonial forests in the Klamath
region were ecologically stabilized because of a combination of indigenous cultural burning (i.e., the intentional application of fire to
land by Native American tribes, tribal organizations, or cultural fire
practitioners to achieve cultural goals or objectives, including subsistence, ceremonial activities, biodiversity, or other benefits) and
lightning ignitions up to colonization (6). Fire behaviors in the
Gila-Aldo Leopold Wilderness Complex in New Mexico and the
Frank Church - River of No Return Wilderness in Idaho have
been shown to be shaped by the presence or absence of prior wildfires (14). Evidence from the 2021 Dixie Fire, the largest single fire
to date in California history at 3900 km2 of burned areas, indicates
strong controls on undesirable fire from past fires that had burned
within its footprint (12). Additional regional studies have shown evidence for the legacy effect of disturbance processes, including the
use of wildfire and prescribed fire, on enhancing fire resilience,
under moderate fire weather conditions (15–17); however, these
studies have been limited to specific geographic subregions and
small numbers of wildfires and have not quantified the magnitude
and duration of the protective effect of such fire.
The goal of this paper is to provide a unified analysis of fire dynamics across California’s forests, based on 20 years of continuous,
satellite-based monitoring of wildfires. We consider data from all
fires detected by satellite monitoring in California’s conifer and
hardwood forests during our study period. Using these data, we
seek to measure whether areas that burn at low intensity are less
likely to experience high-intensity wildfire in the future—and
how long any such protective effect lasts. Operationally, we collect

and harmonize satellite data from various public geospatial data
sources, including Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) Active Fire Products Collection 6.1 (MCD14ML)
(18), Daymet Daily Surface Weather Data V4 data products (19),
California’s Disturbance Agents and Fractional Vegetation Cover
Dataverse (20), and Global Multi-resolution Terrain Elevation
Data (GMTED2010) (21). We categorize every detectable fire
based on its fire intensity, which refers to the energy release of
each fire, and use a physical measurement directly estimated from
MODIS. Fire intensity correlates with, but differs from, fire severity,
which primarily measures above-ground biomass disruptions (see
the Material and Methods for details). We then conduct a counterfactual analysis using the synthetic control approach, a modern, interpretable quasi-experimental design for causal inference (22).
We emphasize that our study measures the overall protective
effect of any low-intensity fires, not just prescribed fires. During
our study period, only 9.5% of wildfires in forests were recorded
as prescribed fires (23), and thus, only considering the effects of recorded prescribed fires would not have given us sufficient statistical
power to measure meaningful effects. The relevance of our study in
informing policy regarding prescribed fire relies on an assumption
that low-intensity wildfire and prescribed fire have similar effects on
forest ecosystems. This assumption has support in the literature: For
example, Taylor et al. (12) find that low-severity wildfires and prescribed fires have similar effects in reducing surface fuels, modifying
the age structure of a forest, and maintaining separation of the tree
canopy from fires with low flame lengths that remain close to the
ground and thus create similar beneficial ecological and fuel treatment effects.
The purpose of fuel treatments is not to exclude all future fires,
but rather to increase the likelihood that, upon the inception of a fire
within a designated area, the fire remains low to moderate intensity
and exhibits a reduced rate of spread. This mitigation strategy provides windows of opportunity for control through the allocation of
additional suppression resources and reduces the potential for the
fire to develop into a high-intensity fire (24). Low- to moderate-intensity fire has beneficial ecosystem effects and is much easier to
manage for the protection of communities and critical

infrastructure. Under the most extreme conditions, even the best
fuel treatments may fail to prevent high-intensity fires with the potential for substantial impacts on both the ecosystem and human
welfare; however, they can increase the chance that fire services
are able to manage their impacts (24). Our purpose here is to quantify the magnitude and duration of potential beneficial impacts of
low-intensity fires in protecting against future high-intensity fires.
RESULTS

To quantify the effect of fire that burned at low intensity on landscapes in a given year, we need to assess how these landscapes might
have evolved had they not burned in that same year and compare
these counterfactuals to their actual evolution. Prior work has
done this using specific examples, e.g., by examining the effect of
past prescribed fires on a subsequent large wildfire in the southern
Cascade Range in California (25), but has not attempted to synthesize evidence across time, multiple geographies, and fuel types. To
facilitate the counterfactual comparisons, we tailor the well-established synthetic control method to create a weighted set of unexposed areas (i.e., synthetic controls) that maintain similar historic
trajectories on fire behaviors and topography, meteorological, disturbance, and vegetation conditions as the exposed area (22, 26, 27).
Figure 1 illustrates the quasi-experimental design under the synthetic control method, in which we divide the time horizons of each
area into three periods: (i) pre-focal period, in which we use pre-exposure covariate trajectories to construct synthetic controls; (ii)
focal period, in which we define exposed and unexposed units
based on fire status during this period; and (iii) evaluation period,
in which we evaluate the impact of fire exposure evolving over years.
We produce the synthetic controls via a covariate balancing weighting algorithm, which is computationally efficient enough to be able
to accommodate large-scale satellite-based data (28–30). We require
a minimal 8-year pre-focal period to allow synthetic controls to be
sufficiently comparable to exposed units in relatively long historic
trajectories, which is why the first focal year starts in 2008.

Results on the magnitude and duration of fire risk
reduction
For the focal period 2008–2020, we found substantial and statistically significant reductions in high-intensity fire risks following
low-intensity fires, although the effect magnitude varies across
land cover type and fire outcome class.
In conifer forests, areas that have recently burned at low intensity
are 64.0% [95% confidence interval (CI): 41.2 to 77.9%] less likely to
burn at high intensity in the following year relative to unburned
synthetic control areas. This protective effect against high-intensity
fires persists for at least 6 years (lower bound of one-sided 95% CI: 6
years). The construction of CIs is described in the Supplementary
Materials. Our findings are robust to whether we consider high-intensity fire events only or combine moderate- to high-intensity fire
events together as the outcomes of interest. On the other hand, the
effect of low-intensity fire in reducing all future fires (of any intensity) is much more muted. In conifer forests, we find a smaller (yet
still statistically significant) 16.4% (95% CI: 2.4 to 28.3%) reduction
in the risk of any fire following low-intensity burn relative to unburned synthetic control areas, and this effect persists for at least
5 years (lower bound of one-sided 95% CI: 5 years).
Our findings in hardwood forests are comparable to those in
conifer forests, except with weaker statistical significance (partially
due to a smaller sample size). We again find that areas burned at low
intensity 1 year earlier are approximately half as likely to burn at
high or moderate-to-high intensity than unburned synthetic
control areas, although (likely due to sample size issues) the result
is not statistically significant for the high-intensity fire outcome. We
are not able to detect any protective effect of low-intensity fires on
overall future fires in hardwood forests.
Figure 2 shows the estimated trajectories of fire risk reductions
up to 9 years following low-intensity fires, stratified by land cover
type and fire outcome class. In the Supplementary Materials, we
show that the effects are robust under sensitivity analyses that
change focal year spans, alter classification schemes for fire intensity, and compare different definitions of fire intensity and severity.
In summary, our finding that low-intensity fire offers years of
protection against future high-intensity fire—but does not necessarily prevent all future fire—is well in line with our knowledge of
the climate and ecosystems in California. It is well understood that
the low-elevation pine and mixed conifer forests in California can
burn at low intensity even just one growing season postfire—and
the ecosystem is well adapted for such frequent, low-intensity
fires (31–33). On the other hand, high-intensity fires happen
when an overgrown understory enables the fire to climb into the
crowns of mature trees (6); and, in this context, our results
suggest that low-intensity fires help, on average across California,
control the amount of available fuels in a way that persists (and protects against high-intensity fires) for many years.
Policy outlook
Our approach illustrates, in the context of California’s forest ecosystems, the benefits of using prescribed fires and managed wildfires
that burn at low intensity as tools to mitigate the risks from highintensity wildfires, i.e., fires of the type that have led to increasingly
adverse impacts on both the ecosystem and human welfare in California and other jurisdictions. Our results allow accurate quantification of the risk mitigation value and duration of investments in
prescribed fire and managed wildfire. We find that the protective

effects of low-intensity fire are strong but decay over the course of
a decade, consistent with existing studies (34–36), implying that
prescribed fire treatments are better thought of as periodic maintenance rather than a one-time intervention for forests that are adjacent to communities or critical infrastructure. To be effective,
investments in restoring low-intensity fire to forest ecosystems in
California need to be structured to recur on a periodic and
ongoing basis.
The findings of this study have important policy implications
related to land management and utility planning. At present, the
U.S. Congress is working on the reauthorization of the U.S. Department of Agriculture programs through the Farm Bill (37). A crucial
aspect of this undertaking involves examining improvements to the
U.S. Forest Service programs governing fuel treatments, and the U.
S. Forest Service has proposed treating nearly 200,000 km2 over the
upcoming decade through a mixture of fuel treatment strategies (9).
Our study provides a quantification of the potential benefit that increased investment in fuel treatments could yield at a scale of geography as large as California. The results of our study provide a
foundation for future evaluation of wildland fuel treatments by
comparing the quantified benefits to potential costs and risks associated with its implementation. It not only illustrates how the intentional reintroduction of low-intensity fire via prescription, cultural
burning, and managed wildfire could potentially lead to a substantial reduction in the occurrence of high-intensity wildfires, improving health, societal, and ecological outcomes for people,
communities, and ecosystems across the western United States
but also illustrates the potential limitations of such a policy if not
sustained over time.
Likewise, California has proposed a substantial expansion of
treated areas, increasing to 2000 km2 annually on state and
private lands (10). Assuming that all such areas were treated with
prescribed fire and that, as suggested by our results, the protective
effects last for at least 5 to 6 years, this implies the state could achieve
ongoing protective effects on the order of 10,000 km2 of forest lands
if the program was sustained. However, given that there are roughly
125,000 km2 of forests in California, of which half are state or privately owned, the risk mitigation benefit of this intervention will
depend heavily on careful selection and targeting of the intervention
to provide maximum protection for people, communities, and
ecosystems.
Our results, by quantifying both the magnitude and duration of
the protective effect of low-intensity wildfire on future high-intensify wildfires, also pave the way for comparing wildland fuel treatments with other forms of wildfire mitigation, such as home
hardening, shaded fuel breaks, or utility ignition avoidance in
terms of both hazard reduction, risk mitigation, and cost-effectiveness. On the basis of our findings, and assuming that low-intensity
fire is a reasonable proxy for prescribed fire, our results suggest a
considerable hazard and risk mitigation effect through the regular
use of prescribed fire. Furthermore, our results indicate a future direction for the evaluation of prescribed fire interventions and a
comparison with other approaches aimed at improving future wildfire outcomes.
While our results highlight the potential benefit of prescribed
fire in mitigating future high-intensity wildfires, they do not
capture the full risk-benefit assessment of prescribed fire. Prescribed fires do occasionally escape prescription and cause widespread losses, as evidenced by recent examples like the Calf

DISCUSSION

This study deployed modern data science tools to quantify the protective effect of low-intensity fire against future high-intensity fire
using unified, large-scale satellite-based data of California’s forests.
We estimate the effects of low-intensity fires via counterfactual
comparisons of areas recently burned at low intensity with unburned synthetic control regions whose past attributes match
those of the burned regions as closely as possible. We find that
low-intensity fires substantially reduce the risk of future high-intensity fires in California conifer and hardwood forests and that this
risk reduction persists for at least 5 to 6 years. This work confirms,

at a large temporal and spatial scale, earlier results from studies of
smaller numbers of wildfires.
This study has several strengths. We collected and harmonized
comprehensive large-scale spatial-temporal data derived from publicly available satellite remote sensing data and we made all code for
processing and analysis of data publicly accessible to ensure reproducibility and transparency of our results. We deployed a synthetic
control methodology to achieve desirable covariate balance for
long-time horizons of covariate trajectories. These methods are
considered a quasi-experimental design that can be used to
measure causal effects from observational data (22). To increase
our confidence in the results, we ran sensitivity analyses using multiple data and model choices. Overall, our study shows how synthetic control methods can be used to leverage large-scale spatialtemporal data in climate and sustainability research.
Our study also has limitations. First, as discussed above, we did
not restrict our analysis to the effects of prescribed fire alone; rather,
we used low-intensity fires as a proxy exposure, which we believe
reasonable since research has shown that prescribed fire mimics
the effects on fuels and ecosystem structure of low-intensity wildfire
(45). In the Supplementary Materials, we also demonstrate that lowintensity wildfires and prescribed fires have similar fire-intensity
distributions. In the future, once we have access to more observations of prescribed fires, it would be interesting to repeat our

analysis to measure the effect of prescribed fires alone. Second, our
quasi-experimental design measured the protective effect of low-intensity fire at a location-by-location level and did not consider spillover effects between neighboring locations. The fact that we use a
relatively large grid size (1 km2) in our analysis partially mitigates
concerns about spillovers; for example, Taylor et al. (16) and Harris
et al. (46) argued that using grids that are at >800 m helps to reduce
the influence of spatial autocorrelation when studying wildfires.
That being said, in future work, it would still be interesting to
refine the analysis using a spatial model that incorporates how
fire propagates across neighboring areas in a landscape. The goal
of improving spatial modeling efforts by incorporating variables
representing neighborhoods was also discussed in Parks et al.
(14), Taylor et al. (47), and Estes et al. (48).
Overall, our study demonstrates that judicious use of prescribed
fire and managed wildfire can offer considerable benefits in protection against future high-intensity wildfires. This finding supports
greater analysis of and investment in the use of prescribed fire
and managed wildfire to alleviate the escalating wildfire crisis in
the western United States. It also contributes to the underlying
benefit quantification needed to estimate risk buydown and cost-effectiveness of reintroduction of fire in California forests, an important area of future research.

A synthetic control study
For each focal year (2008–2020) and forest land cover type (conifer
and hardwood forests), we define an exposed region consisting of all
pixels dominated by that land cover type and that burned at low intensity (class 1 fire) in the given focal year. Pixels that burned at
moderate to high intensity, i.e., with classes 2 to 5 fire, during the
focal year are excluded from the analysis. We then use pixels from
the same land cover type that did not burn in the focal year to form a
synthetic control region, i.e., creating a weighted set of unburned
pixels that, outside of their fire experience in the focal year, look
as similar as possible to the exposed region in terms of historic trajectories—including fire behaviors and topography, meteorological,
disturbance, and vegetation conditions. Last, we can assess the protective effect of low-intensity fire in the focal year by comparing the
evolution of the areas burned at low intensity in the focal year with
their synthetic control region.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We use satellite imagery to assemble a unified fire information
dataset in California 2000–2021. The unit of analysis is a 1-km2
pixel. We define as forests all pixels categorized as either forestlands
or woodlands by the California Department of Forestry and Fire
Protection (CAL FIRE) Fire and Resource Assessment Program
(FRAP) (49), resulting in 124,186 km2 worth of data (we do not disambiguate between forestlands or woodlands in our analysis, as
CAL FIRE separates these landscape types based on land-use
rather than ecological considerations). We further stratify our analysis by vegetation type and separately consider conifer- and hardwood-dominated forests [again as specified in CAL FIRE-FRAP
(49)]; this results in 91,335 km2 of conifer forests and 32,851 km2
of hardwood forests. We do not include grasslands or shrublands
because of their rapid growth after a wildfire.
For each pixel, we obtain a daily estimate of fire radiative power
(FRP) from NASA’s MODIS (50). FRP can be used to quantify fire
intensity; we adapt a classification system that uses FRP to categorize fire intensity into classes 1 to 5 proposed by Ichoku et al. (51).
We denote that a pixel has burned in a given year if its FRP ever
exceeds 0. We denote that a pixel has burned at high intensity if
its maximal FRP in that year exceeds 500 MW (class 3 to 5 fire),
moderate intensity if its maximal FRP exceeds 100 MW but not
500 MW (class 2 fire), and low intensity if its maximal FRP does
not exceed 100 MW (class 1 fire). Table 1 displays the relative frequency of these fire types in California during our study period.
We additionally obtain topography, meteorological, disturbance,
and vegetation from various satellite-based data sources (see the
Supplementary Materials for details) (19, 20, 52). Five meteorological covariates, including minimum air temperature, maximum air
temperatures, precipitation, snow water equivalent, and water vapor
pressure, were chosen as monthly averages to capture the seasonal
variations of meteorological conditions within a year. Other covariates were chosen as the annual averages. Therefore, the analyses

had the exposed regions not burned in the focal year, their average
future fire behavior would have matched that of the synthetic
control region—and thus, any future divergence in fire behavior
for the exposed versus synthetic control regions is due to low-intensity fire in the focal year. The validity of this assumption depends
on, first, the availability of adequate unexposed units for building
the synthetic control region that can steadily track the historic trajectories of the exposed region before the intervention (53) and,

second, there being no unobserved confounders that would lead
to future divergence in the fire behavior between exposed and unexposed synthetic control regions.

design via covariate balance to check the degree to which the distributions of pre-exposure covariate trajectories are similar across exposure and synthetic control regions (54). Figure 3 displays one of

The second point cannot be directly validated from data.
However, we examine the robustness of our results to unmeasured
confounding by calculating E values. The E value for an effect is the
minimal strength of an association, on the relative risk scale, that an
unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure
and outcome, conditional on the covariates already included in the
model, to fully explain the observed association under the null (55).
As discussed in the Supplementary Materials, our main findings
appear to be reasonably robust to unobserved confounding according to this metric.
Last, our analysis also implicitly depends on an assumption that
the only difference in treatment between exposed and unexposed
synthetic control regions is from the exposed regions burning at
low intensity. This assumption could be threatened if California
already had extensive fire mitigation programs in place such that,
e.g., regions that did not burn recently would be prioritized for prescribed burning in the next few years. However, given that prescribed burns are still relatively rare within our data, it is unlikely
this is a major source of bias for our study.
Comparing the fire risks at the post-exposure evaluation period
between exposed units and synthetic controls allows us to assess the
impact of low-intensity fire on subsequent fire behaviors. To investigate long-term effects, we examine a range of evaluation periods,
spanning from 1 to 9 years following the focal year, enabling us to
study the persisting effect of the low-intensity fire exposure for up to
9-year lags. Last, we pool the estimated fire risks at the same lag from
different focal years. We build Wald-type CIs based on standard
errors estimated via a Jackknife variance estimator clustered at the
outcome-year level (56); see the Supplementary Materials
for details.
Fire intensity versus fire severity
In our analysis, we categorize wildfires by the intensity of energy
release as quantified by FRP. The FRP measurement is subject to
measurement uncertainty and errors (57), although we show that
our analysis results are robust to alternate classification schemes
for fire intensity in the Supplementary Materials. Furthermore, in
some settings, it may be more natural to focus on high-severity
wildfires that have transformative ecological impact rather than
high-intensity wildfires with high energy release. In the Supplementary Materials, we apply our proposed method using an ecological
rather than physical categorization of wildfire types. We consider
wildfires assessed as low, moderate, or high severity by the Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity (MTBS) program (42). These assessments capture the degree to which a site has been altered or
disrupted by fire (42). We then seek to measure the extent to
which low-severity fires help prevent future high-severity fires.
While this fire severity definition differs from the fire intensity definition used in this study (and results in a meaningfully different
classification of fires), the high-level finding that prescribed fire
has the potential to prevent future fires with potentially substantial
impacts persists. Notably, the magnitudes of resulting protective

# Supplementary content

S.1 Data
S.1.1 Overview
Table S1 summarizes the data that we collected from various publicly available data sources. All
of the data are satellite-based, while using different algorithms to extract information from satellite
imagery, thus having different spatial and temporal resolutions. Detailed descriptions of each data
source are provided in the subsequent sections.

S.1.2 Fire and Fire Intensity
We collect fire and fire intensity data from the National Aeronautics and Space Administration
(NASA) Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS) (50). The FIRMS dis-
tributes both near real-time and standard science quality active fire data processed by NASA’s
Science Computing Facility using satellite observations from NASA’s Moderate Resolution Imag-
ing Spectroradiometer (MODIS) and Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS). In this
paper, we use the MODIS Collection 6.1 active fire products (MCD14ML), given that it provides
standard science quality data from earlier years (starting November 1, 2000). Each MODIS active
fire/thermal hotspot location represents the center of a 1-km2 pixel flagged by the algorithm as con-
taining one or more fires within the pixel. Combined (Terra and Aqua) MODIS active fire products
are processed using the standard MOD14/MYD14 Fire and Thermal Anomalies algorithm (18).
In addition to information on active fires, MODIS also measures energy release from open-air fire
burns, including the brightness temperature (in Kelvin) and the fire radiative power (FRP) of each
fire pixel (in 1 km2). Satellite-derived FRP observations, as a measure of fire prevalence and inten-
sity, allow distinction between fires of different strengths over multiple years (51). Note that fire
pixels in MCD14ML are not defined by an Earth-fixed coordinate reference system (CRS).

We define fire intensity classes using satellite FRP measurements devised by Ichoku et al. (51).
Specifically, the scheme classifies each 1-km2 pixel using MODIS measurements of the annual
maximal FRP as follows: class 1 (< 100 megawatts (MW)), class 2 (100 to < 500 MW), class
3 (500 to < 1000 MW), class 4 (1000 to < 1500 MW) and class 5 (≥ 1500 MW). Based on the
classification scheme above, we define that a 1-km2 pixel has burned in a given year if its FRP
ever exceeds 0. We define that a pixel has burned at high intensity if its maximal FRP in that year
exceeds 500 MW (Category 3-5 fire); moderate intensity if its maximal FRP exceeds 100 MW but
not 500 MW (Category 2 fire); and low intensity if its maximal FRP does not exceed 100 MW
(Category 1 fire). We define exposed and control units by the fire status during a focal period (i.e.,
a given year). We assign pixels that only encounter class 1 fires during a focal period as exposed
units, whereas pixels without fire burns during a focal period will belong to the control region (see
Section S.2 for additional details).



S.1.3 Topography Data
We collect topography data (e.g., elevation) across California from the Global Multi-resolution
Terrain Elevation Data (GMTEDmd2010) at 1-km2 resolution. It is known that topography impacts
fire behaviors (58).

S.1.4 Meteorological Data
We collect meteorological data from Daymet Daily Surface Weather Data V4. Daymet is a research
product created by the Environmental Sciences Division at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Oak
Ridge, TN (19). Daymet provides long-term, continuous, gridded estimates of daily weather and
climatology variables by interpolating and extrapolating ground-based observations through sta-
tistical modeling techniques. Daymet meteorological variables include daily minimum and max-
imum temperatures, precipitation, snow water equivalent, and water vapor pressure produced on
a 1-km2 gridded surface over continental North America and Hawaii from 1980 and over Puerto
Rico from 1950 through the end of the most recent full calendar year. In this paper, we obtain
monthly summaries of all five meteorological variables, including minimum air temperature, max-
imum air temperatures, precipitation, snow water equivalent, and water vapor pressure, starting
from January 2000 to December 2021 in California.

S.1.5 Disturbance Data
We collect annual data on types of disturbance (e.g., fire, harvest, drought, and forest die-off) that
occur in California from the Dataverse “Disturbance Agents in California, 1985 - 2021” (20). The
data mapped annual disturbances in California from 1985 to 2021 using time-series remote sensing
and geospatial data. Estimates are derived by applying a change detection algorithm to time series
of Landsat data and using machine learning, calibrated against existing geospatial datasets, to clas-
sify detected breaks. The data contain different types of disturbance, including fire, timber harvest,
drought-induced forest die-off, unattributed greening, and unattributed browning. Greening and
browning refer to events that trigger a statistical change in remote sensing time series. Fire, har-
vest, and die-off disturbances are of relatively high severity. In this paper, we use all five different
types of disturbance to characterize a relatively complete disturbance history of each pixel.

S.1.6 Vegetation Data
We collect annual data on fractional vegetation cover from the Dataverse “Fractional vegetation
cover in California, 1985 - 2021” (20). The data mapped annual vegetation cover changes in Cal-
ifornia from 1985 to 2021 using time series remote sensing and geospatial data. While fractional
cover (0 - 100%) data for four vegetation types, including tree, shrub, herbaceous, and bare, are
available, we focus on tree covers to characterize the canopy cover of each pixel, as the canopy
cover shapes fire behaviors.

We also use a vegetation classification scheme that aligns with the California Department of
Forestry and Fire Protection (CAL FIRE) Fire and Resource Assessment Program (FRAP). Ac-
cording to the CAL FIRE website, CALFIRE-FRAP compiled the “best available” land cover data
into a single data layer. The FRAP grouped vegetation classes into 10 major land cover types based



on the California Wildlife Habitat Relationships (CWHR) Classification Scheme. In the analyses,
we focus on the following forest types: (1) conifer forests (lands with conifer >10% cover) and (2)
hardwood forests (lands with hardwood >10% total cover and conifer <25% total cover).

S.1.7 Fire Severity
We collect data on fire severity from the Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity (MTBS) and Rapid
Assessment of Vegetation Condition after Wildfire (RAVG) programs. These programs focus
mainly on the impact or degree of ecological change caused by a fire in a particular area, mea-
sured as fire severity. In particular, MTBS maps the burn severity and extent of large fires across
all lands of the US from 1984 to the present, including all fires 1000 acres or greater in the western
US and 500 acres or greater in the eastern US. RAVG includes data estimating post-fire vege-
tation conditions on National Forest System (NFS) lands, including basal area loss and canopy
cover loss from 2012. Detailed descriptions of the MTBS and RAVG programs can be found
on their government websites, https://www.mtbs.gov/ and https://burnseverity.
cr.usgs.gov/ravg/, respectively.

Both fire intensity and severity are key concepts used in understanding and assessing wildfires (59).
They refer to different aspects of a fire, and each plays an important role in the management and
study of wildfires. Fire intensity generally refers to the amount of energy or heat output during a
wildfire over a specific area and time. It is a measure of the power or energy release of the fire.
The intensity of a fire will influence its behavior - for example, higher-intensity fires might spread
more rapidly or create more extreme conditions. Fire intensity can be influenced by factors such
as fuel load, wind speed, temperature, and humidity. Fire severity is used to describe the impact
of a fire on an ecosystem. It generally refers to the degree of environmental damage caused by a
fire. Fire severity can be influenced by fire intensity, but it also depends on other factors like the
resilience of the ecosystem, the type of vegetation, and the duration of burning.

S.1.8 Prescribed Fire
We collect geospatial prescribed data from both state and federal sources. The state’s prescribed
fire burn data are available from CAL FIRE. The refined archival forest management records of
the Forest Activity Tracking System (FACTS) of the US Forest Service (USFS) are available from
a dataset published by Knight et al. (23). Knight et al. (23) refined the spatial and temporal
details of the aggregated forest management record using the Continuous Change Detection and
Classification (CCDC) algorithm on satellite remote sensing data to produce a state-wide time
series map of harvest disturbances.

S.1.9 Data Linkage
MODIS fire products exhibit grid shifts in their multi-day outputs. In other words, fire pixels from
different days do not maintain consistent centroids. Hence, to accurately reconstruct the time series
of fire information at fixed locations, an Earth-fixed terrestrial reference system becomes necessary
to fix grid cells. We use the uniquely defined coordinate system WGS 84, which is recognized
as the standard definition for a global reference system by the US Department of Defense for



geospatial data and serves as the reference framework for the Global Positioning System (GPS).
The chosen coordinate system was produced as rasters at 30 arc-second resolution, i.e., equivalent
to 1-km2 grid cells at the equator. Relying on the chosen coordinate system, we create fixed-
location daily time series of fire and fire intensity for each 1-km2 grid cell.

Spatial data from additional sources have different spatial and temporal resolutions, as well as
different systems of coordinates. We geographically intersect topographic, meteorological, distur-
bance, and vegetation data, aligning location and time on the same coordinate system, WGS 84.
We assign spatial data for topography and meteorological variables at 1-km2 resolution using the
greatest intersection with the 1-km2 fixed grid cells to join the grid cells with different coordinates
(see the upper panel of Figure S1). For spatial data for disturbance and vegetation variables with
a smaller spatial resolution (30 m2), we use zonal statistics to calculate their aggregated values
based on all observations that are intersected with the 1-km2 grid cells (see the lower panel of
Figure S1).

S.2 Statistical Methods
S.2.1 Synthetic Controls
This section briefly summarizes the statistical ideas underlying our approach to creating and assess-
ing counterfactual comparisons. The following section describes the adopted covariate balancing
synthetic control approach and its implementation (28-30). The synthetic control method, initially
proposed by Abadie et al. (22,27), has rapidly become highly influential in a number of fields,
especially in the social sciences (60). Briefly, the synthetic control method is a statistical method
that constructs a weighted combination of unexposed units used as controls to which the exposed
group is compared. This comparison is used to estimate what would have happened to the exposed
group if it had not been exposed (i.e., a counterfactual outcome comparison). The synthetic con-
trol method can be seen as a quasi-experiment that generalizes from the difference-in-difference
method, with a number of advantages (26,61). One of the major advantages is that synthetic control
can account for the effects of potential confounders changing over time by weighting the control
group to better match the exposed group prior to the exposure. It is also one of a small number of
methods that may control for unmeasured time-varying confounders (62).

The synthetic control method deployed in this study uses time series data containing more than
twenty years of covariate trajectories. We divide the time series into three periods (shown in Figure
1 of the main text). Specifically, 1) the pre-focal period serves the role of constructing the weights
for the unexposed units to create a set of synthetic controls that mirrors the exposed units as closely
as possible; 2) the focal period defines the exposed or unexposed group (e.g., choosing the exposed
units as the pixel only having low-intensity fires within the given year); 3) the evaluation period
provides the outcomes comparisons between the exposed group vs. synthetic controls to estimate
what would have happened to the exposed group if it had not been exposed.

We use the following notations: Let N denote the total number of 1-km2 forested pixels in Cali-
fornia, and T denote the total number of years in the time series. We define the focal year as T0

and, by definition, 1 < T0 < T . For pixel i = 1, 2, . . . , N , let Wi,T0 be the binary exposure to low-
intensity fire as of the focal year T0, and Xi,t be the set of pre-focal covariate trajectories (t < T0).



To ensure rigorous and maximal control of covariate trajectories, Xi,t includes the monthly average
of each meteorological and fire behavior (frequency, brightness, and intensity) covariates. Let Yi,t

be the outcome of interest in year t among the evaluation period (T0 < t < T ), and Yi,t(w) be the
counterfactual outcome had the i-th unit received exposure w ∈ {0, 1} at time T0. The stable unit
treatment value assumption (SUTVA) implies that Yi,t = Yi,t(Wi,T0) given the observed exposure
Wi,T0 . Our goal is to estimate the average causal effect among the exposed (ACEE), which is aver-
aged over pixels in the exposed region for the given focal year T0, for each year t > T0. Denoting
year lag τ = t− T0, we define

and the year lag τ . The quantity measures the lagged effect of the occurrence of low-intensity fires
in the focal year T0 on future fire risks τ years later on a relative scale. We focus on the ratio of
two average counterfactuals since the relative risk (RR) directly measures the percentage change
in fire frequency, a more useful measure in wildfire mitigation.

S.2.2 Covariate Balancing by Tailored Loss Functions
One key consideration of the synthetic control methods is ensuring that the synthetic controls are
similar to the exposed units regarding pre-exposure covariate trajectories. We use covariate bal-
ance to quantify the degree to which the distributions of pre-exposure covariates are similar across
exposure and control units (63). This concept initially arises from randomized experiments. In
randomized experiments, researchers randomly allocate the study units across different exposure
levels and thus ensure the exposure variable is independent of all pre-exposure covariates. In other
words, randomization eliminates any systematic differences in pre-exposure covariate distributions
between exposure and control units (63), making randomized experiments the “gold standard” for
estimating the causal effect of an exposure on an outcome of interest (64). Whereas in observa-
tional data, due to lack of randomization, the covariate trajectories between exposure and control
units could be substantially different (i.e., imbalanced) (63). Therefore, synthetic control methods
assign weights to a combination of control units to achieve covariate balance between exposure
units vs. synthetic controls.

The original synthetic control approach seeks to eliminate confounding by balancing out past tra-
jectories in the outcome of interest (in our case, fire behavior). In our setting, however, balancing
fire behavior alone may not be enough to fully eliminate confounding—and we also seek to balance
out the spatial distribution and meteorological histories between the exposed and control regions.
To accomplish this, we cast our synthetic control problem with extended balance conditions as a
covariate balancing problem using the unconstrained convex optimization formulation proposed
by Zhao (30). The step-by-step implementation is given below.

Setup. Assume that the analysis is based on the focal year 2008. For each pixel i and year t,
we collect a set of d-dimensional covariates Xi,t ∈ Rd. We further create a vector Xi stacking
horizontally the time-varying covariates Xi,t that characterize a pixel’s complete trajectory during



the pre-focal period (2000-2007):

Xi = [Xi,1,Xi,2, . . . ,Xi,t] (t < T0).

Meteorological data were chosen as monthly averaged because 1) data at daily resolution are too
noisy and increase the total covariate dimension of Xi substantially (×30 times compared to data at
monthly resolution); whereas, 2) the annually averaged data do not capture the seasonal variations
of meteorological conditions within a calendar year. The monthly averaged covariates capture
the granularity of the temporal variations in the specific study context. Other covariate data were
chosen as the annual averages.

Shown in Table S2, the dimension of the covariates Xi is d = 553 for the balance condition needed
for the analysis on the focal year 2008, and the dimension of the covariates further increases to

control approach could handle. To solve this large-scale optimization problem, we adopt covariate

S.2.3 Pooling Estimates
The synthetic control approach described above can be used to produce RR estimates for each pair
of year lag and focal year (τ, T0), representing the effects of low-intensity fires at focal year T0 on
the wildfires τ years later. In some years with many wildfires, these individual RR estimates can
be moderately useful in and of themselves; however, in other years, these estimates are too noisy
to be interpreted on their own.

Figure S2 displays all the individual RR estimates we obtained for conifer forests and the high-
intensity (class 3-5) fire outcome, with each individual estimate scaled to reflect its precision and
color-coded by the outcome year. California experienced major fire seasons in both 2020 and 2021,
so we can examine how likely areas that burned at low intensity in 2020 were to reburn at high
intensity in 2021 to get a reasonably good estimate of the strength of 1-year fire protection: 10
pixels that burned at low-intensity in 2020 re-burned at high intensity in 2021, whereas a weighted
18.5 synthetic control pixels that didn’t burn in 2020 then burned at high intensity in 2021, resulting
in a RR estimate of 54% (this datapoint is color-coded in blue in Figure S2 since the outcome was
measured in 2021). On the other hand, 2015 and 2016 had lighter fire seasons, and so the same
exercise comparing these two years results in much noisier RR estimates: 1 pixel that burned
at low intensity in 2015 re-burned at high intensity in 2016, whereas a weighted 0.35 synthetic
control pixels that did not burn in 2015 burned at high intensity in 2016, resulting in a (noisy and
implausible) RR estimate of 281%. Individual RR estimates for other land cover types and fire
outcome classes are available in our GitHub replication repository.

To improve power and interpretability, we pool estimates across different values of the focal year
T0 and lag τ via log-linear linear modeling. We start by specifying a log-linear model,

log (ACEEτ,T0) = α + βτ. (5)



This log-linear model captures two assumptions: That the protective effect of fire does not depend
on the start year T0 and that the log-protective effect decays linearly with time. Generally, in this
model, we expect α to be negative (i.e., low-intensity fire has an immediate protective effect) and
β to be positive (i.e., this effect decays). Clearly, in the limit where lags are infinitely long, any
effects of past fire must vanish—i.e., limτ→∞ ACEEτ,T0 = 1, and so the relationship (5) must fail
for large values of τ . However, the model (5) fits our data well in the estimation range, i.e., with
τ ∈ [1, 9].

We fit the parameters α and β in (5) via weighted quasi-Poisson regression, using the R function
glm (65). Specifically, we fit the functional form (5) to the individual synthetic control RR esti-
mates as displayed in Figure S2, and weight each of these individual RR estimates by the number
of burned pixels in the synthetic control regions (note that the number of burned synthetic control
pixels corresponds to a prediction of the number of re-burned pixels we should observe if there
were no treatment effect). In Figure S2, the resulting pooled estimate (for conifer forests and the
high-intensity fire outcome) is shown as a thick black curve.

Point-wise confidence intervals (CIs) for ACEEτ were obtained via a Wald-type construction with
jackknife standard errors (56). To account for the fact that fire behavior was highly idiosyncratic
across years, and the fact that a given pixel might—for a given outcome-year—be used as both a
treated pixel for one lag and contribute to the synthetic control for a different lag, the jackknife
was clustered at the outcome-year level (i.e., all observations with the same value of t = T0 + τ
were considered to be in the same cluster for the purpose of jackknifing). In the context of the
illustration in Figure S2, the jackknife removes all individual RR estimates of the same color, and
then examines how stable the synthesis line is to removing these clusters of points.

S.3 Additional Analysis Results
S.3.1 Confidence Intervals on Persistence of Protective Effects
Following the construction of one-sided CIs in Section S.2.3, we obtain one-sided 95% CIs for
ACEEτ shown in Figure S3. We then construct a one-sided CI for effect persistence as (τ0, +∞),
where τ0 + 1 is the smallest value at which the one-sided CI for ACEEτ includes a RR of 1.
For example, in the lower left panel of Figure S3, this results in a lower endpoint of a 95% CI
for persistence of 6 years. The validity of this confidence interval construction can be verified
by recognizing that hypotheses regarding effect persistence are naturally nested (i.e., if an effect
persists τ years then it must also persist for τ ′ years for any τ ′ ≤ τ ), and so we can invoke the
closed testing procedure argument (67).

S.3.2 Covariate Balance
As mentioned in Section S.2, the covariate balancing weighting approach allowed us to assess the
quality of the approach by evaluating the covariate balance of the weighted data.

First, we apply a threshold < 0.1 for absolute standardized mean differences to indicate an accept-
able covariate balance. Table S3 shows the number and percentage of individual covariates with an
absolute standardized mean difference > 0.1 (imbalanced) by different land cover types and focal



years. The main analysis accounts for up to d = 1, 383 pre-exposure covariates, including fire
behavior history, topography, meteorological, disturbance, and vegetation variables. We found that
without using synthetic control weights, the exposed and unexposed regions are highly imbalanced
with approximately 10%-65% of covariates above the threshold, whereas after weighting, all co-
variates are below the threshold for all combinations of land cover types and focal years, indicating
excellent performances of the covariate balancing approach.

Second, we visually check the overall covariate balance for each focal year from 2008 to 2020
across different land cover types (conifer and hardwood forests). The upper panels of Figures S4-
S15 show standardized mean differences for the average of each covariate time series. We observed
that most of the covariates in the pre-focal period are largely imbalanced in the original unweighted
data, which indicates a substantial risk of confounding bias in the unweighted data. After imple-
menting synthetic control methods for covariate balance, covariate balance has improved signifi-
cantly in all settings, regardless of the choice of focal years and land cover types. These covariate
balance results indicate the high quality of synthetic controls constructed to facilitate counterfac-
tual comparisons to estimate the effects of low-intensity fires on future fire intensity. We used the
covariate balancing approach to balance the whole covariate trajectories with the granularity at the
month or year level for the entire pre-focal period. For visual conciseness, we present the average
standardized mean differences for each time-varying covariate across the entire time span.

In the lower panels of Figures S4-S15, we also visualize the geographic distributions of observed
fires (i.e., the exposed region) and their corresponding synthetic control regions for each focal
year across different land types. It is critical that the synthetic control is built from a donor pool
of potential controls that are similar to the exposed region. We found that most of the selected
controls are in the neighborhood regions of exposed units, indicating the geographic similarity of
the exposed region and control pools.

S.3.3 Unmeasured Confounding
We perform additional analyses to evaluate the robustness of the results to unmeasured confound-
ing by calculating the E-value. The E-value for the main effect of interest is the minimal strength of
an association, on the relative risk scale, that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with
both the exposure and the outcome, conditional on the covariates already included in the model, to
fully explain the observed association under the null (55). The E-value for the 95% CI upper limit
is the minimal strength of an association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have for
the 95% CI to include a null effect.

The E-values for the results reported in the main text are given in Figure S16. For example, in
the lower-left panel, we see that the point estimate of the 1-year protective effect of past class 1
fires on class 3-5 fires in conifer forests has an E-value of 5. This means that, in order to eliminate
the estimated relationship between past class 1 fires and class 3-5 fires, an unobserved confounder
would need to modify the relative risk of both past class 1 fires and class 3-5 fires by a factor of
5. In other words, any unobserved confounder would have to have a very strong association with
both the exposure and outcome variables to cancel out this finding—and thus, our result appears to
be reasonably robust to unobserved confounding. Moreover, as seen in other panels, the E-values
for our claimed statistically significant results are generally large throughout. In contrast, we did



not claim to detect a strong effect of past class 1 fires on future class 1 fires; in fact, the associated
E-value for this effect is small.

The calculation of E-value is implemented using the EValue R package by Mathur et al. (68).

S.4 Sensitivity Analyses
We conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our results to design and data choices.

S.4.1 Focal Year Span
We consider the robustness of our results to the range of focal years used for our analysis. Fig-
ures S17 and S18 show the RRs of fire frequency for exposed units vs. synthetic controls ag-
gregated across different ranges of focal years. The main analysis summarized the results across
thirteen focal years, 2008-2020. Here, we present the results aggregated from 2006-2020 and
2010-2020. We found consistent results showing the persisting protective effect of low-intensity
fires on future high- and moderate-to-high-intensity wildfires.

S.4.2 FRP Measurement Error
It is well known that satellite-based FRP measurements are subject to measurement error; see, e.g.,
Freeborn et al. (57). To assess sensitivity to potential measurement error, we conduct an alternative
analysis in which we construct our fire intensity metric by averaging multiple FRP measurements,
with the idea that this construction could also help average out the errors from individual FRP
measurements. Rather than utilizing the peak FRP in a given year, we compute the mean of the
three highest (top-three) FRP values within a given year to categorize each pixel’s fire intensity.
We characterize a pixel as having undergone a high-intensity burn if the average of its top-three
FRP exceeds 500 MW, which corresponds to a Category 3-5 fire. If the average of the top-three
FRP exceeds 100 MW but not 500 MW, we classify it as a moderate-intensity fire or Category
2 fire. Lastly, we designate a pixel as a low-intensity burn or Category 1 fire if the mean of the
top-three FRP does not exceed 100 MW. As shown in Figure S19, we found that the results are
very similar to the main analysis where we used the maximal FRP to classify fire intensity for each
pixel in each focal year, indicating our findings appear robust to measurement error in individual
FRP measurements.

S.4.3 Comparison to Fire Severity
To assess sensitivity to our choice of outcome metric, we compare the results by FRP measurement
with fire severity metrics by the MTBS and RAVG programs. As shown in Table S4, we found
similar year-by-year patterns of annual burn areas for the study period captured by different fire
intensity or severity measurements. RAVG only contains data from 2012 forward and records
fewer fires than the other two measures. We hypothesize that this is because RAVG only produces
data on National Forest System (NFS) lands. We also find that many low-intensity fires captured by
FRP measurements are not captured by the MTBS program. Additionally, there are meaningfully



different classifications of high-intensity vs. high-severity fires. The fact that the FRP-MTBS
categorizations do not show perfect alignment is not surprising, as fire intensity and fire severity
are different (albeit related) ways to quantify the magnitude of a fire (the former is a physical
measurement, whereas the latter is an ecological assessment), and each plays an important role in
the management and study of wildfires.

Finally, we repeat our full analysis using the fire severity measure of the MTBS program, as op-
posed to FRP measurements, to construct both our treatment and outcome variables. Shown in
Figure S20, we found similar patterns of protective effects of low-severity fires on future fires, es-
pecially those burned on moderate and high severity. Notably, we observed much more pronounced
effects using fire severity measured by MTBS than the findings in the main analysis, where we used
the fire intensity measured by MODIS. We hypothesize that this difference is because the MTBS
only includes fires with visible alteration of vegetation, dead biomass, and soil and large fires that
burned 1000 acres or greater in the western US.

S.4.4 Prescribed Fires
We investigate what would happen if we used only prescribed fires—as opposed to all low-intensity
fires—as our treatment. We collect two geospatial prescribed fire data from the USFS’ FACTS and
CAL FIRE. We geographically overlay these two prescribed fire data onto satellite-based active fire
data. Given the fuzziness of time and location in FACTS and CAL FIRE archival forest manage-
ment records and actual fires identified by satellite remote sensing data (23), we use the following
criteria to overlay two fire polygons (recorded prescribed fires vs. actual fires): 1) they are geo-
graphically intersected and 2) they take place in the same year. We found that only 8,682 (5.4%)
active fires match those recorded prescribed fires from CAL FIRE or FACTS and thus have been
considered confirmed prescribed fires. Among different land cover types, 9.5% of low-intensity
wildfires in forests are prescribed fires, whereas in conifer forests, 10.6% are prescribed fires,
and in hardwood forests, 5.0% are prescribed fires. In terms of intensity, we found that among
both conifer and hardwood forests, low-intensity wildfires and prescribed fires have similar fire
intensity distributions, although low-intensity wildfires appear to be still slightly more intense than
prescribed fires (see Figure S21).

Since the prescribed fires confirmed by existing archival forest management records consist of
only 9.5% of the low-intensity fires among California’s forests, it is severely underpowered to run
analyses using only those confirmed prescribed fires as the exposure, and overly large CIs could
diminish any meaningful findings. We presented the results using confirmed prescribed fires only
as an illustration (see Figure S22). As expected, the CIs for all groups of land cover types and fire
outcome classes were dramatically increased. Consequently, any scientific interpretations drawn
from the figures should be approached with caution. The similarity of fire behaviors between
confirmed prescribed fires and low-intensity wildfires, and the rareness of prescribed fires leads us
to use low-intensity wildfires as a proxy exposure of interest.

Due to the similarity in fire behaviors between confirmed prescribed fires and low-intensity wild-
fires, coupled with the infrequent occurrence of prescribed fires (i.e., lead to sample size issues),
we have opted to utilize low-intensity wildfires as the exposure of interest.
