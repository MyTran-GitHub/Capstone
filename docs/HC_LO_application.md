# High Competency (HC) and Learning Outcome (LO) Application in the Capstone Project


## Extensively Applied High Competencies (HCs)

This project demonstrates deep and practical application of the following five HCs:


### 1. Hypothesis Development
**Application:** The project began with the explicit formulation of hypotheses about how embedding-based donor selection would affect both pre-treatment fit (RMSPE) and ATT estimates. This process was not static: I first articulated a causal mechanism—embedding-based restriction would yield tighter pre-fit and possibly shift ATT—then designed the analysis pipeline to test these claims. As results emerged, I revisited and refined my hypotheses, for example, recognizing that improved RMSPE did not always translate to a change in ATT. This iterative process involved: (1) stating clear, testable hypotheses; (2) designing diagnostics and figures (e.g., RMSPE and ATT distributions) to test them; (3) interpreting results in light of the original hypotheses; and (4) transparently documenting hypothesis revision in the manuscript. This approach ensured that the project was driven by scientific reasoning, not just data exploration, and that all claims were grounded in empirical evidence and subject to revision as new insights emerged.


### 2. Gap Analysis
**Application:** A thorough gap analysis was foundational to the project’s methodological innovation. I systematically reviewed the literature and existing pipelines (e.g., in wildfire SCM studies), identifying that most approaches relied on observed covariates or simple distance metrics, which often failed to capture latent ecological similarity. I compared these methods to the needs of my research question, documenting their limitations in both the introduction and methods sections. This analysis led to the adoption of satellite-derived embeddings and clustering for donor pool restriction—a direct response to the identified gap. The process included: (1) mapping existing solutions and their limitations; (2) justifying why these limitations mattered for causal inference in wildfire policy; (3) proposing and implementing a novel solution; and (4) evaluating its impact through diagnostics and figures. This gap analysis was not just a literature review, but an active, iterative process that shaped the project’s design and justified its contributions.



### 3. Correlation
**Application:** Correlation analysis was rigorously applied at multiple stages to validate the ecological relevance of the embedding space and to support interpretability. Specifically:

- **Embedding–Covariate Correlation:** I computed R² values and Pearson correlation coefficients between embedding dimensions and key environmental covariates (elevation, canopy, precipitation), using both linear regression and correlation matrices. For example, “We quantified the explanatory power of embeddings by regressing environmental covariates on embedding vectors, reporting high R² values... which demonstrates strong correlation and supports the interpretability of the embedding space.” (see Results, Problem 5). Scatter plots and heatmaps were used to visualize these relationships, and high R² values (e.g., 0.86 for elevation) provided quantitative evidence that embeddings captured meaningful ecological variation.

- **Similarity–Outcome Correlation:** I also examined the correlation between embedding-based similarity scores and pre-treatment outcome similarity, assessing whether ecological similarity translated into better pre-fit. This analysis informed the choice of donor pool restriction strategy and was visualized with scatter plots and correlation coefficients.

- **Interpretation and Limitations:** For each correlation, I carefully considered the possibility of spurious associations, confounding, and the limits of correlation (avoiding causal claims). The results were used to justify embedding-based donor selection, address “black-box” critiques, and guide further analysis. This multi-step, evidence-based application of correlation ensured that the project’s methodological choices were both transparent and scientifically justified.



### 4. Distributions
**Application:** Distributional analysis was a cornerstone of both the diagnostic and inferential workflow, with each key metric analyzed in depth:

- **Pre-treatment RMSPE:** I plotted the full distribution of pre-treatment Root Mean Squared Prediction Error (RMSPE) for both baseline and embedding-based donor selection methods. This included side-by-side boxplots and kernel density estimates (see Figure 4). The analysis revealed that the embedding-based approach consistently produced a tighter (lower variance) RMSPE distribution, indicating more reliable pre-treatment fit across treated units. I also examined outliers and the tails of the distribution to identify units where fit was poor, using this to refine control pool definitions and optimization settings.

- **ATT (Average Treatment Effect on the Treated):** I compared the distribution of ATT estimates under both control pool strategies, visualizing not just the mean but the spread and skewness (see Figure 3). This allowed me to assess whether embedding-based selection shifted the central tendency or reduced the variability of treatment effect estimates. The results showed that while the mean ATT was similar, the variance was reduced, supporting the claim that improved donor selection yields more stable inference.

- **Similarity Scores:** I computed and plotted the distributions of cosine similarity, Mahalanobis distance, and Euclidean distance between treated and control units. Histograms and density plots (see Figure 1) were used to assess overlap and to justify the choice of cosine similarity for final donor pool restriction. This analysis provided evidence that embedding-based clusters captured deeper ecological similarity than pairwise metrics alone.

- **Covariates:** For key covariates (e.g., elevation, canopy cover), I visualized the distributions for treated units, embedding-selected controls, and the full control pool using boxplots and density plots (see Figure 5). This allowed for a direct assessment of covariate balance, demonstrating that embedding-based controls more closely matched the treated group than the baseline pool. I also examined the impact of donor pool restriction on the tails and central tendency of covariate distributions, providing evidence for improved ecological comparability.

For each metric, the process included: (1) selecting the most informative visualization (e.g., boxplot for spread, density plot for shape); (2) justifying visualization parameters (bin width, color, axis limits); (3) interpreting features such as central tendency, spread, skewness, and outliers in the context of causal inference; (4) using distributional diagnostics to refine hypotheses, analytic choices, and reporting; and (5) transparently documenting all findings in the manuscript. This comprehensive, metric-specific approach to distributions provided deep, actionable insight into method performance and supported robust, nuanced inference.



### 5. Significance
**Application:** The project applied statistical significance testing at multiple decision points to ensure that observed improvements were not due to random variation. Specifically:

- **Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test:** I used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to compare paired pre-treatment RMSPE values for each treated unit under baseline and embedding-based donor selection. This non-parametric test was chosen to avoid normality assumptions and to directly test whether embedding-based selection consistently improved pre-fit. As documented: “I applied the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to the paired pre-treatment RMSPE values for each treated unit... The results showed a highly significant p-value (p < 0.001), indicating that, across nearly all treated units, the embedding-based method yielded lower or equal pre-treatment RMSPE than the baseline.” (see Results, Problem 5).

- **Permutation and Placebo Tests:** In addition to the Wilcoxon test, I designed and interpreted permutation and placebo-based inference procedures to assess the robustness of ATT estimates and to construct confidence intervals. These tests provided further evidence that observed effects were not due to chance or outlier units.

- **Interpretation and Reporting:** For each test, I formulated clear null and alternative hypotheses, selected appropriate significance levels, and interpreted p-values in the context of both statistical and practical significance. Effect sizes and confidence intervals were reported where appropriate, and all results were transparently documented in the manuscript. This rigorous, multi-method approach to significance testing ensured that all claims were robust, reproducible, and meaningful for both scientific and policy audiences.
